There is a pattern I have seen in scaling organisations.

A decision moves between departments.

Nobody is entirely sure who owns it.

Two teams interpret the priority differently.

A dependency is missed.

The issue escalates.

And eventually it lands with the CEO.

The CEO resolves it.

For a while, the business moves again.

Then another issue arrives.

And another.

Eventually the CEO becomes the organisation's integration layer.

This often looks like strong leadership from the outside.

Inside the organisation, it can be a warning sign.

The CEO should be spending disproportionate time on direction, choices, capital, people and the most consequential decisions.

If the CEO is repeatedly reconciling departmental priorities, chasing updates, resolving routine hand-offs and reminding leaders about commitments, the organisation may have an operating-rhythm problem.

This is one of the reasons I think a good CEO Office is often misunderstood.

It should not exist simply to create presentations.

It should not become another approval layer.

And it should not become a shadow management team doing the work of functional leaders.

Its value is in helping the organisation convert leadership intent into coordinated execution.

That can mean creating an Annual Operating Plan that is actually connected to operating commitments.

It can mean establishing a Rhythm of Business where leadership reviews focus on decisions and deviations rather than presentations.

It can mean clarifying which issues belong inside a function and which require cross-functional ownership.

It can mean ensuring that an important commitment does not disappear between two departments.

It can mean preparing the CEO to make a decision quickly because the trade-offs have already been made visible.

And sometimes it means telling leadership that what appears to be a functional problem is actually an organisational one.

The strongest CEO Offices reduce dependency on themselves over time.

They build mechanisms.

Clear priorities.

Decision rights.

Review rhythms.

Escalation paths.

Ownership.

<div class="pull-quote"><p>The goal is not to sit at the centre of every conversation. The goal is to make fewer conversations require the centre.</p></div>
