Transformation programmes often begin with the visible object.

A new ERP.
A new CRM.
A new workflow tool.
A new data layer.

The assumption is that the organisation is fragmented because its technology is fragmented.

Sometimes that is true.

But often the technology is only where the fragmentation becomes visible.

At Addverb, the obvious symptoms appeared across functions: Procurement was struggling with planning, Design changes were not flowing cleanly, Manufacturing was working with inconsistent information, and After-Sales repeatedly depended on other teams to resolve customer issues.

It would have been easy to call this an ERP problem.

It wasn't.

The deeper issue was that different parts of the company were operating with different versions of truth.

A flagship robot could have multiple Bills of Materials in circulation. Design decisions travelled through emails and individual conversations. Procurement could buy against information that had already changed. Manufacturing did not always receive approved design information in a structured way. After-Sales had limited independent capability.

The software landscape reflected the operating model.

That distinction matters.

<div class="pull-quote"><p>If you digitise a broken process without first deciding how the process should work, you often create a faster, more expensive version of the same confusion.</p></div>

So before selecting technology, I prefer to ask a different set of questions.

What is the current operating process?

Where are decisions made?

Where does information change hands?

Who owns the truth at each stage?

Where is manual judgement useful, and where is it simply compensating for a broken system?

Which hand-offs repeatedly fail?

What should the future state actually look like?

Only after the AS-IS becomes visible can the TO-BE be designed properly.

And only after that does the technology decision become meaningful.

This is also why transformation is rarely an IT programme.

It changes ownership.

It changes the way functions interact.

It changes who is accountable.

It changes the information leaders can see.

It changes the work people have been doing for years.

That is why some of the hardest moments in a digital transformation are not technical.

They are human.

A senior leader disagrees with the new workflow.
A department worries that it is losing control.
A team is being asked to document knowledge that previously lived in people's heads.
An old workaround suddenly becomes impossible.

These are not side effects.

They are the transformation.

The technology matters enormously. But it should sit inside a redesigned operating system rather than become the operating system by default.
