Startups are taught to persist.

Improve the product.
Find more customers.
Sell harder.
Raise more capital.
Scale.

Persistence matters.

But sometimes the harder question is whether the company is commercialising the right part of what it has built.

At ClearMeat, the long-term ambition was cultivated meat.

But the end consumer market was not yet ready for the product in the way a conventional consumer business needs it to be.

The technology journey, regulation, product readiness and market economics were all still evolving.

Meanwhile, the company already had upstream capability.

Cell-growth media and related ingredient technology had applications beyond one future consumer product.

That changed the strategic question.

Instead of asking:

**How do we sell cultivated meat faster?**

the better question became:

**Which capability we already own is commercially useful today?**

That opened different customers.

Research laboratories.

Pharma.

Food-tech companies.

Academic institutions.

Different customers meant a different sales motion.

A different business model.

Different partnerships.

And critically, revenue while the larger category continued to develop.

This is an important distinction for founders.

<div class="pull-quote"><p>A company's original product vision and its first scalable revenue engine do not always have to be the same thing.</p></div>

The key is not pivoting randomly.

It is understanding which assets are genuinely differentiated, who values them, and whether commercialising them strengthens or distracts from the long-term advantage.
